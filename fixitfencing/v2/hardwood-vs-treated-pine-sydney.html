Hardwood vs Treated Pine: which timber lasts longer in a Sydney backyard?

A side-by-side breakdown of the two timbers we install most often — what they cost, how long they last, and which one belongs in your yard.

Written byAdmin15 May 20263 min read
Share Share on Facebook Share on X Share on LinkedIn Share by email

Most of the timber fences we build in Sydney come down to two choices: F7 treated pine or a hardwood like spotted gum, blackbutt or merbau. Both have their place. Neither is automatically better. The right call depends on your block, your budget, and how long you want the fence to look new versus how long you want it to simply stand up.

This is the rundown we give every customer who asks — straight off the truck, no marketing.

What you are actually paying for

A timber fence is really three jobs stacked on top of each other: posts, rails, and palings. The posts do the structural work, the rails carry the palings, and the palings are what you see. Pine and hardwood behave very differently in each of those roles.

Treated pine is dimensionally stable, lightweight, and friendly to a nail gun. It is also pressure-treated to H4 in-ground rating, which means the timber is impregnated with copper-based preservatives that fight rot and termites. A pine post in clay-heavy Sydney soil, set properly in concrete, will typically give you 15–18 years before the bottom 200mm starts to soften.

Hardwood, by contrast, is denser, heavier, and slower to dry out. A spotted gum post bedded the same way can sit in the ground for 25 years and still take a hit from a mower without flinching.

The honest cost comparison

For a standard 1.8m lapped-and-capped fence run of 20 lineal metres, here is roughly what we quote in 2025:

  • Treated pine: $185 to $215 per metre, installed
  • Mixed hardwood palings on pine rails: $245 to $280 per metre
  • Full hardwood: $310 to $370 per metre

The full-hardwood price covers a fence that, with a quick re-oil every four or five years, will still look respectable in 2050. The pine price gets you a fence that looks the same on day one but will likely need palings replaced from year 12 onward.

The cheapest fence is rarely the cheapest fence. It is the one you have to rebuild in eighteen years.

Where each one belongs

Pine is the right answer when:

  • The fence is between two neighbours and cost is being split
  • You plan to stain or paint it within the first six months
  • The site has good drainage and the soil is sandy or loamy

Hardwood earns its premium when:

  • The fence is street-facing or sits in a landscape feature
  • You want the timber to silver naturally without being painted
  • The site sits low and stays damp for weeks after rain
  • You are not planning to live with a stain-and-oil schedule

A note on the in-ground bit

Whichever timber you go with, what saves the post is what happens at ground level. We always concrete posts with a slight crown so water runs away from the timber, and we use Z-flashing under the lowest paling to keep splash-back off the bottom edge. Skip those two steps and even the best merbau will rot from below within ten years.

If you are not sure which way to go, send us a couple of photos of where the fence will run and a rough idea of your budget. We will tell you straight — sometimes the answer is “do the front in hardwood and the side return in pine” and you save four figures without losing the look.
